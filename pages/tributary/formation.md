## The Birth of Sediment

Sediment does not simply exist—it becomes. In the red sandstone cliffs of Glen Canyon, ancient seas compressed into stone begin their slow return to particle and grain. Wind carves crescents in canyon walls. Water finds cracks, freezes, expands, splits stone into smaller and smaller pieces.

This is where sediment begins: in the patient work of weathering, the daily cycles of heat and cold, wet and dry, that gradually transform solid rock into the mobile materials that will travel through the ecosystem.

### Mechanical Weathering

The most visible form of sediment creation in Glen Canyon happens through mechanical weathering—the physical breakdown of rock without chemical change. Temperature fluctuations cause rocks to expand and contract, creating stress fractures. These fractures become pathways for water, which freezes in winter and expands with tremendous force, wedging apart even the most solid-seeming stone.

> "The canyon walls are not walls at all, but slow waterfalls of stone, grain by grain returning to the river that will carry them onward."

### Chemical Weathering

Less visible but equally important is chemical weathering, where the mineral composition of rocks changes through interaction with water, oxygen, and other chemicals. The iron-rich minerals in Glen Canyon's sandstone oxidize, creating the distinctive red coloration while simultaneously weakening the rock's structure.

### Biological Contribution

Living things participate in sediment formation too. Lichens secrete acids that dissolve rock surfaces. Plant roots grow into cracks, applying pressure as they expand. Even bacteria contribute to the chemical processes that transform stone into soil and sediment.

## Scales of Formation

Sediment formation happens across multiple scales of time and space:

- **Geological time**: The formation of the rocks themselves, compressed over millions of years
- **Human time**: The weathering processes we can observe over decades
- **Daily cycles**: The expansion and contraction that happens with each day's temperature change
- **Microscopic**: The molecular-level processes of chemical weathering

Understanding sediment formation means learning to see across these different scales, to perceive the mountain becoming grain, the cliff face becoming the river's burden and gift.

## Indigenous Perspectives on Formation

Indigenous knowledge keepers have long understood the dynamic relationship between stone and sediment, recognizing landscapes as living systems in constant transformation rather than static backdrops. This understanding offers crucial insight into thinking about formation not as destruction but as transformation—part of the ongoing life of the land.
