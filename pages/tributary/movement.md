## Sediment in Motion

Once freed from stone, sediment begins to travel. Its movement is not random but follows the physics of water, wind, and gravity, creating patterns that connect distant places in the ecosystem. A grain of sandstone weathered from a canyon wall might travel in a stream, settle in a backwater, be picked up again by spring floods, and eventually find its way to Lake Powell's depths.

### Modes of Transport

Sediment moves through the landscape in several ways:

**Suspension**: Fine particles float within moving water, creating the muddy appearance of the Colorado River during flood season. These suspended sediments can travel enormous distances before settling.

**Bedload**: Larger particles roll, slide, and bounce along the bottom of streams and rivers. The sound of bedload movement—stones clicking against each other—is one of the river's more subtle songs.

**Saltation**: Medium-sized particles hop along the riverbed or ground surface, picked up by current or wind, carried a short distance, then dropped to be picked up again.

**Wind transport**: In this arid landscape, wind plays a crucial role in moving fine sediments, sometimes carrying particles hundreds of miles from their origin.

## The River as Conveyor

Before the construction of Glen Canyon Dam, the Colorado River moved enormous quantities of sediment—an estimated 80-90 million tons per year flowed past the future dam site. This massive movement of material connected the entire Colorado Plateau, carrying nutrients and minerals from the Rocky Mountains to the Gulf of California.

> "To follow sediment is to trace the arteries of the landscape, to understand how distant places speak to each other through the language of moving stone."

### Seasonal Rhythms

Sediment movement follows the seasonal rhythms of the watershed:

- **Spring snowmelt**: High water carries the year's largest sediment loads
- **Summer thunderstorms**: Flash floods mobilize sediment from side canyons
- **Fall and winter**: Lower flows allow sediment to settle and sort
- **Drought years**: Reduced flow concentrates existing sediments

## Patterns of Deposition

Sediment doesn't just move—it also stops, creating new landforms and habitat:

- **Point bars**: Sediment deposits on the inside of river bends
- **Backwaters**: Slow-moving areas where fine sediments settle
- **Deltas**: Fan-shaped deposits where tributaries meet the main river
- **Beaches**: Sandy areas that provided camping and nesting sites before the dam

### Size Sorting

Moving water sorts sediment by size and density. Faster currents carry larger particles; as flow slows, particles settle out in order of size—boulders first, then cobbles, gravel, sand, and finally silt and clay. This natural sorting creates diverse habitats and substrates throughout the river corridor.

## Interrupted Journeys

The construction of Glen Canyon Dam fundamentally altered sediment movement patterns. The reservoir now traps nearly all incoming sediment, preventing it from reaching downstream areas that depend on these regular deliveries of new material. This interruption has cascading effects throughout the ecosystem.

## Wind and Aeolian Transport

In Glen Canyon's arid environment, wind plays an important role in sediment movement. Fine particles dried on newly exposed shorelines can be lifted into dust storms, carrying sediment far from its river origins into neighboring ecosystems and even human communities.
